The rules (there are only three, we promise)

  1. 1
    Pick a number from 0 to 100. Any number. Decimals welcome.
  2. 2
    We take the average of everyone's guesses.
  3. 3
    The winner is whoever is closest to ⅔ of the average.
🏆
The prize: eternal glory, valid in both Ann Arbor and Kalamazoo. Ties split the glory. Glory is non-rival, so nobody gets less. Ask your econ professor.

    How deep did you think? Level-k reasoning

    Level 0 doesn't think about anyone else and picks at random, so the average guess is 50.

    Level 1 assumes everyone else is level 0, so they guess ⅔ × 50 ≈ 33.

    Level 2 assumes everyone else is level 1, so they guess ⅔ × 33 ≈ 22.

    Each extra level multiplies by another ⅔. Keep going forever and you hit 0.

    Still a sensible guess: 0 to 100

    Even if everyone guessed 100, the target would be ⅔ × 100 ≈ 66.7. So any guess above 66.7 can never win. Cross it out.

    Nash equilibrium: everyone guesses 0.
    If everyone else picks 0, your best response is 0 too. Nobody wants to deviate, and that's exactly what makes it an equilibrium.

    The catch: you only get here if everyone is rational, knows everyone is rational, knows that everyone knows… all the way down. Economists call it common knowledge of rationality. Undergrads call it "a lot."

    Being right isn't enough

    Guessing 0 is "correct" in theory, but it only wins if everyone else got there too. You win by being exactly one step ahead of the crowd, not infinitely ahead.

    Keynes's beauty contest

    Keynes compared investing to a newspaper beauty contest where you win by picking the face others will pick. Markets reward predicting the crowd, which is how bubbles can form.

    Other rooms

    In Rosemarie Nagel's 1995 lab experiments, first-round averages landed in the mid-30s. In Richard Thaler's 2005 Financial Times contest, the winning guess was 13.

    Round 2: now that you know the theory…

    Will the room march toward 0?

    Economists call this learning. Students call it "the professor tricked us again." Each round, players see the last result and best-respond to it, so the target usually falls.

    Why it works

    Every student plays, and the room's own data become the lecture. Nobody can zone out of a contest they might win.

    What it teaches

    Best responses, dominated strategies, Nash equilibrium, bounded rationality, and why asset prices can drift from fundamentals.

    Variations

    Change ⅔ to ½ or 1.1. Play before and after the theory. Let a "trader" team coordinate. Compare sections.

    The setup

    A tiny web server, a QR code, and phones. Students play at /play; the teacher deck lives at /teacher.
