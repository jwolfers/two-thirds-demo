The rules

1
Pick a number from 0 to 100. Decimals allowed. Overthinking encouraged.
2
We compute the average of everyone's guesses.
3
The winner is whoever is closest to ⅔ of the average. Ties share the glory.
Target = ⅔ × average guess

The prize: bragging rights. Which, coincidentally, is also what Michigan football is playing for this season.

Finding the Nash equilibrium

So why didn't we all say zero?

Rationality isn't enough. Zero requires that everyone is rational, knows everyone is rational, knows that everyone knows… all the way down. That's common knowledge of rationality.
The best guess is a best response to what others actually do. If the room thinks one or two steps, the winner thinks one step more, and no further.
Keynes's beauty contest. Markets reward guessing what others will think others will think. Being right too early is just being wrong.
Average guess, ⅔ game

Nagel (1995, AER), lab subjects, first round. Financial Times readers' contest (Thaler, 1997). Approximate.

Run it back

Play a second round and the average almost always falls. People learn what the room does, and best-respond to that. Keep playing and you converge on the Nash equilibrium.

Repeated play: also the reason we keep watching Michigan football. Hope springs eternal.

Steal this game

Play before you teach. Losing to ⅔ of the room makes Nash equilibrium personal.
Ask who said more than 66.7. There's always someone. That's your dominated-strategy lecture, delivered by a volunteer.
Play round two cold. Watching the average fall is learning, in real time.
Change the rules. Try ½ the average, or the median, or students vs. faculty.
Build your own. This whole app (the phone page, this deck, the server) came from a one-paragraph description, written with an AI coding assistant. If you can describe a classroom game, you can build it.
