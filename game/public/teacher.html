The rules easier than Michigan's playbook

1

Pick a number

Any number from 0 to 100. Decimals welcome.

2

We average them

Then take two-thirds of that average. That's the target.

3

Closest wins

Whoever is closest to ⅔ × average wins. Ties share the glory.

Example If the room averages 45, the target is ⅔ × 45 = 30, and the person who picked closest to 30 wins.

The prize: bragging rights. Which, as a Michigan fan, I can tell you are in short supply this year.

How deep did you think? level-k reasoning

The Nash equilibrium iterated elimination of dominated strategies

So why didn't we all say zero?

Nash needs more than smarts

Zero requires that everyone is rational, knows everyone is rational, knows everyone knows it… all the way down.

Winning ≠ being "most rational"

The winner best-responds to the room as it is. Be one step ahead of the crowd, not infinitely ahead.

Keynes saw it in 1936

Markets reward anticipating what average opinion expects the average opinion to be.

Newspaper contests: Bosch-Domènech, Montalvo, Nagel & Satorra (2002), "One, Two, (Three), Infinity, …", American Economic Review.

Round two: now that you know…

Now everyone has seen where the room landed. What happens next round?

In the lab, guesses fall fast with repetition and head toward zero (Nagel, 1995). Learning does what introspection can't.

Unlike Michigan's season, this one gets better the more we play.

Written with an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) from a plain-English description of the class I wanted to teach.
