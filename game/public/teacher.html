How we picked the winner

Level-k thinking: how many steps ahead?

    Nash equilibrium: eliminate, repeat

    So why didn’t we all say zero?

    Rational ≠ common knowledge of rationality

    Zero needs you to be rational, and everyone else to be rational, and everyone to know that everyone knows… all the way down. One level-0 player in the room breaks the chain.

    The equilibrium answer isn’t the winning answer

    Your best response is ⅔ of what the room actually guesses. You want to be exactly one step ahead of the crowd, not infinitely ahead of it. Being right too early is just being wrong.

    Keynes’s beauty contest (1936)

    Investors don’t pick the stock they think is best; they pick the one they think others will think is best. “We have reached the third degree”… and some practise the fourth and fifth.

    Average guess, first time played
    Nagel (1995) lab subjects; Financial Times readers’ contest (Thaler, 1997) had a winning number of 13. Play again and watch this room slide toward zero.

    Steal this game

    Play it twice. The second round shows learning, and convergence toward Nash, in real time.
    Ask before revealing: “What do you think the average was?” Students who guess the crowd well are doing level-k on the fly.
    Change the multiplier. Try p = 1/2, or p > 1, and the Nash equilibrium flips to 100.
    Mix the room. Faculty vs. freshmen, or one section vs. another. Who thinks more steps ahead?
    Connect it. Asset bubbles, Keynes’s beauty contest, coordination games, strategic thinking in oligopoly.
    Build your own. This whole app is a few hundred lines of code, written with an AI assistant. Your next classroom game is an afternoon away.
